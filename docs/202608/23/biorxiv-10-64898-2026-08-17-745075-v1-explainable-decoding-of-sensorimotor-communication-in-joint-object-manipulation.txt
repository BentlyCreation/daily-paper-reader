Abstract 1

Humans often collaborate under asymmetric information, for example when two people carry a table 2

and only one knows the destination. They coordinate without speech using cues from movement kinematics, 3

interaction forces, and object states. Characterizing this sensorimotor communication is difficult because 4

these signals both execute the task and convey information, whose meaning is context-dependent. Here, 5

we investigated a virtual table-carrying task where one partner knew the target while the other inferred 6

it from visuo-haptic feedback. Participants flexibly adapted kinematic and haptic cues across contexts to 7

convey intention. We introduce an explainable machine-learning framework that decodes intent from ongoing 8

multimodal signals and quantifies where individual features are informative. Incorporating the decoded 9

signals into a drift-diffusion model accurately predicted the uninformed partner’s target choices and decision 10 

times. Together, our framework explains how humans communicate through action and offers principles for 11 

collaborative robots to infer and express intent through physical interaction. 12 13 

Humans excel at physical collaboration, coordinating seamlessly by continuously predicting and adapting to their 14 

partners’ actions [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8], while forming internal representations of their partners’ behavior [9, 10, 11]. 15 

Achieving fine coordination requires partners to learn each other’s movement intentions, which becomes especially 16 

challenging under asymmetric information , when one partner lacks full knowledge of the environment (e.g., the 17 

goal or an obstacle) and must infer it from their partner’s behavior. Despite this information gap, partners can 18 

often coordinate effectively without words, communicating intentions through action by modulating movements 19 

and forces, a process referred to as sensorimotor communication [12]. Effective communication may be facilitated 20 

by legible behavioral signals that enable observers to infer intent quickly and confidently [13]. Although central 21 

to human physical collaboration, the mechanisms by which movement and force jointly convey intent remain 22 

largely unknown. 23 

Sensorimotor communication emerges intuitively in humans [14], yet its underlying structure is difficult to 24 

characterize. One reason is its inherently multimodal nature: it relies on high-dimensional kinematic and haptic 25 

signals [15] such as movement path, speed and force magnitude. These signals are physically coupled and often 26 

covary, yet they remain dissociable and can diverge, for example during collisions. Consequently, changes in one 27 

variable may reflect intentional signaling, mechanical task constraints, or indirect consequences of modulating 28 

another variable. Moreover, actions in physical collaboration simultaneously serve the task goal and convey 29 

intentions and task-relevant information. Communicative signals are embedded within task execution and must 30 

respect the behavioral constraints imposed by the task. Together, these properties make human sensorimotor 31 

communication difficult to interpret. 32 

Previous studies show that humans enhance legibility by exaggerating or disambiguating movements relative 33 

to alternative goals. For example, they modulate movement trajectories [16, 17, 18, 19], velocity profiles 34 

[20, 21], grip aperture [17], movement duration [22], and movement variability [23, 24] (see [12] for a review). 35 

Communicative signals are often identified as deviating from energetically optimal movements observed when 36

Haptic feedback is a critical communication channel during physical collaboration [29, 30]. Humans use 41 

haptic cues to infer a partner’s movement intentions [10, 31], which can improve interpersonal coordination 42 

[32, 33] and task performance [34, 35]. These effects depend on factors such as the coupling stiffness [34], 43 

individual performance [36], individual motor costs [37], and role distribution within the dyad [32, 33, 38]. In 44 

prior studies, haptic feedback arose naturally from physical coupling during task execution, but the tasks did 45 

not require participants to actively shape haptic signals to enhance legibility because both partners had full 46 

visibility and faced no environmental constraints. Moreover, it remains unclear how haptic signals interact with 47 

visual cues during active communication. 48 

Here we examine how intention is encoded in and decoded from multimodal sensorimotor signals during 49 

physical collaboration. In complex collaboration, partners may embed intention in force, movement, and 50 

object-state signals, creating a high-dimensional signaling space that is potentially redundant, as the same intent 51 

can be expressed through different combinations of signals. Meanwhile, the informativeness of each signal may 52 

depend on the task structure, local environment, and concurrent signals. We ask three questions. First, how is 53 

intent embedded across multimodal sensorimotor signals as a function of contextual factors? Second, does intent 54 

expression show consistent patterns within individuals, and are these patterns shared across participants? Third, 55 

how can we decode and interpret the information conveyed through sensorimotor communication? 56 

To study multimodal sensorimotor communication, we used a joint transport task in which pairs of participants 57 

moved a virtual table freely in a plane toward one of four possible targets while receiving visual and haptic 58 

feedback. This setup created a rich set of cues through which partners could communicate flexibly, without 59

1.2 Multimodal signaling 120 

In this joint manipulation task, partners were mechanically coupled through the object, creating a potentially 121 

rich physical channel for communication. In principle, the informed partner could embed target information 122 

in multiple variables, such as the table’s path, orientation, or interaction forces. To characterize how behavior 123 

changed under asymmetric information, we compared these variables across conditions. The Symmetric condition 124 

was used as a baseline, as it reflects efficient transport without the need for signaling. Deviations from this 125 

baseline in Asymmetric and Button Press conditions may indicate adjustments to improve legibility. 126 

Dyads collaborated to transport the table in two scenes with different target layouts (Fig. 3D). Asymmetric 127

paths leading to nearby decoys. Furthermore, deviations were generally larger in the Button Press condition 129

conservative modulation of the path is consistent with the absence of increased dyadic work under asymmetric 132 

information (Fig. 3B), suggesting that cues other than path deviations may be used to convey target information. 133 

We then examined differences in table orientation, table velocity, and interaction forces between the three 134 

conditions. Table orientation profiles showed significant differences between conditions after the second passage. 135 

Deviations from the Symmetric baseline emerged in the Asymmetric condition and were even more pronounced in 136 

the Button Press condition (Fig. 3E). Table velocity also differed across conditions (Fig. 3F), with the Symmetric 137 

condition exhibiting higher speeds than the Asymmetric and Button Press conditions. Finally, leaders applied 138 

higher forces in the Button Press and Asymmetric conditions, particularly between the two passages (Fig. 3G). 139 

Comparing the two scenes further revealed that signaling adjustments depended on local environmental 140 

context rather than target location alone. T 1, T 2, and T 4 had identical positions across scenes, whereas T 3141 

differed in location. Thus, the same spatial location could correspond to a different local context with different 142 

nearby decoys. For instance, in Scene 1, T 2 lay between T 1 and T 3, leading to minimal deviations from the 143 

Symmetric baseline as shifts in either direction would have been misleading. By contrast, in Scene 2, T 2 had the 144 

same location but a different local context, with only one competing decoy ( T 1) on the upper right, leading to 145 

downward shifts in path and orientation. Another example was T 4 after the second passage: leader force showed 146 

little difference across conditions in Scene 1, since no decoys were present. However, in Scene 2, leader force 147 

increased under asymmetric information, possibly to further enhance separation from T 3. Conversely, targets 148 

with similar local contexts showed similar signaling strategies despite different locations. T 3 in Scene 1 and T 2149 

in Scene 2 both had competing decoys only to the upper right, and both showed similar downward adjustments. 150 

On the other hand, deviations from the Symmetric baseline cannot always be attributed to sensorimotor 151 

signaling. As shown in Fig. 3C, information asymmetry shifted more work share toward the leader, which could 152 

cause behavioral changes. For example, before the second passage, table orientation was more tilted in the 153 

Asymmetric and Button Press conditions (Fig. 3E), which could result from the leader dominating the maneuver, 154 

or from table tilt being used as an informative signal, or both. Similarly, the lower speed observed in the 155 

Asymmetric and Button Press conditions likely arose because one partner did not know the target, preventing 156 

the dyad from pushing together consistently, rather than because speed itself served as a signal (Fig. 3F). 157 

These results, together with comparisons of other sensorimotor signals (see Supplementary Note 3), suggest 158 

that sensorimotor signaling is multimodal, high-dimensional, and context-dependent. However, behavioral 159 

differences under asymmetric information may not always reflect signaling alone, motivating the use of more 160 

powerful tools to decode it. 161 

1.3 Machine learning decoding of sensorimotor communication 162 

Decoding the target from the instantaneous movement state requires combining multiple cues whose meaning 163 

can be context dependent. These cues interact non-linearly, which complicates the interpretation of their 164 

combined meaning using classical methods. Therefore, we used a Random Forest (RF) classifier to implement 165 

context-dependent decision rules by partitioning the feature space, interpret sensorimotor cues, and output 166 

probabilities for each target. The probabilities reflect the model’s confidence in each target as the intended goal, 167 

and thus the legibility of the instantaneous sensorimotor signals. 168 

The spatial distribution of the Random Forest model’s confidence for each target in Scene 2 is shown in 169 

Figure 4A, with the corresponding results for Scene 1 shown in Supplementary Note 4.1. At the start of 170 

trials, confidence was near chance level ( ∼ 25%), as the initial trajectories were similar across targets and thus 171 

uninformative. Confidence increased as the table moved toward the target, with some regions reaching higher 172 

confidence earlier than others. In the top-right room, confidence rose faster for T 1 along the scene’s upper 173 

boundary and for T 2 within the lower part. In the bottom-right room, T 3 was positioned near the passage and 174 

along the route to T 4. Accordingly, T 3 had high confidence only within a small area around the target, whereas 175 

for T 4, confidence sharply increased even before the table passed T 3, suggesting clear signaling beyond position 176 

cues around T 3. Confidence dropped sharply when the table position was more consistent with a decoy, as visible 177

The RF model’s confidence in the true target is plotted as a function of the table’s normalized horizontal position, 182 

where 0% corresponds to the second passage and 100% to the target location. Confidence started at similar 183

In addition to the general RF model trained on pooled data from all dyads, we also trained dyad-specific RF 190 

models on each dyad’s data to assess their individual signaling strategies. The dyad-specific models were evaluated 191 

on a shared dataset aggregated from all dyads, and their predictions were compared using the Jensen-Shannon 192 

(JS) distance. 193 

The mean pairwise JS distance between each pair of dyads and each dyad compared to the general model is 194 

shown in Figure 4C, where smaller JS distances indicate more similar strategies. To characterize how dyads 195 

differed in their signaling strategies, we applied agglomerative hierarchical clustering with average linkage based 196 

on the JS distance. Dyads that clustered earlier and appeared lower in the dendrogram were more similar to 197 

one another, whereas dyads that clustered higher showed more distinct strategies. Moreover, we applied metric 198 

multidimensional scaling (MDS) to the JS distance matrix, embedding dyads in a three-dimensional space that 199 

approximately preserves their high-dimensional distances (stress=0.124, Fig. 4C inset). Dyads located closer 200 

together showed more similar signaling strategies, whereas those farther apart exhibited more distinct strategies. 201 

Overall, JS distances remained small and spanned a narrow range (0.08–0.14), indicating that most dyads 202 

had similar signaling strategies. A few dyads showed larger distinction to both the general model and other 203 

dyads. In particular, D9, D1, and D2 exhibited the largest JS distance, appeared at the top of the dendrogram, 204 

and also appeared at the edge of the MDS map. This indicates comparatively distinct strategies. Conversely, 205 

dyads near the bottom of the dendrogram (e.g., D10, D15, D3, and D12) were closer to the general model and 206 

to most other dyads, suggesting more common signaling strategies. 207 

Importantly, distinct signaling strategies do not imply poorer coordination. For instance, D9 achieved the 208 

earliest button presses, and D1 pressed at the smallest distance from the start (see Supplementary Note 6). 209 

Therefore, some dyads may have developed individual “dialects” that facilitated more efficient communication. 210 

1.4 SHAP-based interpretation of model decisions 211 

The Random Forest model compresses high-dimensional sensorimotor states into target confidence, but like 212 

many machine-learning models, its decision process is not directly interpretable. Therefore, we used SHAP 213

1.5 Modeling the follower’s decision-making process 246 

In the Button Press condition, the uninformed participant gradually accumulated evidence from the ongoing 247 

sensorimotor signals and pressed a button once they identified the target, providing empirical decision times. 248 

The broad distribution of table positions at the time of the button presses (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Note 5) 249 

reflected a large variability in signaling strategies, cue interpretation, and reaction time. The relatively rare 250 

incorrect choices (marked as ×) tended to occur in spatially ambiguous regions, where samples associated with 251 

different targets overlapped. 252 

To model this decision-making process, we applied the widely used Drift-Diffusion Model (DDM) framework. 253 

In its classical form, DDM assumes a one-dimensional evidence signal. Thus, it is not directly applicable to our 254 

task, where signals are high-dimensional and must first be interpreted before being treated as evidence. Therefore, 255 

we combined the DDM with the RF decoder. Specifically, we used the RF to map the high-dimensional feature 256 

state to one-dimensional time-varying confidence traces for each target. These traces were then used as inputs 257 

to the DDM, which accumulated positive evidence when confidence for a target exceeded chance level (0.25 for 258 

four targets), and negative evidence otherwise. We implemented a racing DDM in which four accumulators, one 259 

for each target, accumulated evidence in parallel. For each trial, the first accumulator to reach the decision 260 

bound predicted the target choice of the uninformed participant, with its crossing time predicting the decision 261 

time. One racing DDM was fit for each participant using the Button Press trials in which that participant was 262 

uninformed. 263 

A representative trial illustrates the DDM process, with both the confidence inputs and the corresponding 264 

accumulated evidence traces (Fig. 6B). Early in the trial, confidence stayed near chance level for all targets, 265 

leading to near-zero evidence accumulation. As informative cues emerged, RF confidence increased for the 266 

target ( T 1) and decreased for the decoys. Correspondingly, T 1’s accumulator rose and crossed the positive 267 

bound, whereas competing accumulators drifted downward. The predicted decision time closely matched the 268 

participant’s button press time. 269 

Across participants, the racing DDM predicted the participant’s chosen target with an accuracy of 94 .9% ±5.1% 270 

(mean ±SD). Predicted decision times closely matched empirical ones, with points clustered around the unity 271 

line (Fig. 6C). Prediction errors were small and approximately unbiased (0.02 ±0.37 s; Fig. 6C, inset), indicating 272 

little systematic bias. That is, the DDM could accurately predict both the selected target and decision time of 273 

the participants for each trial, suggesting that the RF model effectively captured the target-relevant information 274 

carried by the sensorimotor signals. 275 

Decision-making models such as the DDM provide a mechanistic account of how choices are formed over 276 

time, offering insight into the latent computations that give rise to behavior. By combining the RF model with 277 

a racing DDM, we extended evidence accumulation modeling to high-dimensional sensorimotor signals, in which 278 

the relevant evidence is not directly available but must first be inferred. The high prediction accuracy further 279

# 2 Discussion 281 

Physical collaboration often requires partners to combine their available information and coordinate their actions 282 

despite unequal access to task-relevant information. In this study, we investigated how humans with asymmetric 283 

information convey intent through sensorimotor signals during joint object manipulation. We introduced a unified 284 

analysis pipeline that bridges high-dimensional behavior with cognitive decision-making: decoding intent from 285 

sensorimotor signals, extracting the context-dependent semantics of specific kinematic and kinetic features, and 286 

modeling the evidence accumulation process. Through these analyses, we demonstrate that intent is not conveyed 287 

by a single channel, but through the flexible modulation of multimodal kinematic and haptic cues. Critically, 288 

increasing legibility did not necessarily come at the cost of transport efficiency. Humans selectively modulated 289 

the sensorimotor features that were most informative within the local task geometry, without increasing total 290 

mechanical work. Moreover, while most dyads exhibit a shared signaling consensus, we observed the emergence 291 

of dyad-specific “dialects” that potentially yielded more efficient communication. 292 

Prior works have often studied legibility in joint action through visual cues, such as the trajectory shape 293 

[16, 22], leaving the contribution of haptic signals to legibility less explored. In contrast, our results show that 294 

intent is conveyed through integrated multimodal cues that combine visual and haptic signals. Importantly, 295 

these communicative cues are embedded within actions that must remain pragmatic for task completion. Within 296 

this constraint, dyads flexibly shifted which features they relied on, or combined multiple cues, depending on 297 

the local workspace geometry and which decoys were currently plausible. This context-dependent multimodal 298 

signaling may have allowed dyads to make their actions legible without additional energetic cost. 299 

Several studies have proposed that deviations from efficient movement should be a core mechanism for intent 300 

communication [16, 25]. Consistent with this idea, observers often interpret inefficient actions as attempts to 301 

convey information [41, 42, 43]. However, this view has largely been developed in tasks with limited action 302 

redundancy, where only a small number of efficient alternatives are available to achieve the goal. In such settings, 303 

communicative modulation may necessarily appear as deviations from efficiency. By contrast, in our joint object 304 

manipulation task, participants increased movement legibility without compromising efficiency. Joint object 305 

manipulation involves multimodal, high-dimensional sensorimotor signals, creating natural redundancy in how 306 

the task can be completed. Participants could therefore select more legible actions from among similarly efficient 307 

alternatives, and introduce communicative modulations that either contributed to task completion or carried 308 

little additional energetic cost. This may have allowed dyads to communicate intent without compromising 309 

dyadic efficiency. 310 

We found a shared consensus on sensorimotor signaling strategies across the majority of dyads. This ‘universal 311 

language’ could arise from the task’s physical constraints as well as participants’ past experiences in joint actions, 312 

reflecting shared principles through which communicative meaning is embedded in, and inferred from, movement. 313 

Such shared principles may provide a foundation for human physical collaboration, allowing even unfamiliar 314 

partners to rapidly establish mutual understanding and coordinate their actions. However, as dyads repetitively 315 

worked together, we observed the emergence of dyad-specific ‘dialects’ characterized by systematic deviations 316 

from the general strategy. These dialects potentially enabled pairs to improve communication. This suggests 317 

that sensorimotor communication is both robust and adaptive, relying on broadly shared baseline strategies that 318 

can be further specialized as partners converge on dyad-specific features. 319 

Importantly, we introduced in this paper a framework to decipher the high-dimensional sensorimotor 320 

communication by integrating (i) the predictive power of machine learning (Random Forest), capturing statistical 321 

regularities in sensorimotor communication, (ii) the transparency of explainable AI (SHAP), quantifying the 322 

relative contribution of each feature to the information carried by sensorimotor signals, and (iii) models of 323 

human decision making from cognitive neuroscience (Drift-Diffusion Models), simulating the process of evidence 324 

accumulation and decision-making. A challenge in computational modeling of human behavior is the tension 325 

between model complexity and interpretability: more complex models can capture richer behavioral structure, 326 

but are often harder to interpret. Traditional computational approaches in motor control often favor compact, 327 

interpretable models, such as Bayesian inference or optimal control frameworks, which offer deep mechanistic 328 

insights and clear conceptual narratives. However, these models can become computationally costly, even for 329 

seemingly simple and constrained tasks. For example, action understanding has been formalized as Bayesian 330 

inverse planning [44], and later extended to Bayesian theory-of-mind models [45], but scaling such models to 331

pronounced in sensorimotor signaling, where the meaning of an action depends on a complex interplay between 333 

multiple coupled variables, the local geometry, and uncertain predictions of the partner’s responses. 334 

To address these limitations, modern work in computational neuroscience increasingly integrates machine 335 

learning models to handle complex and large-scale data [46, 47]. Our framework leverages Explainable AI as 336

across diverse contexts. Moreover, our pipeline extended Drift-Diffusion Models from accumulating evidence over 339 

one-dimensional stimuli to high-dimensional sensorimotor states. In this way, we provide a unified computational 340 

framework for studying how humans communicate through sensorimotor signals. 341 

Robotics research often treats generating legible motions as an optimization problem to maximize a legibility 342 

function [48], where legibility is defined with hand-specified efficiency and rationality rather than learned from 343 

human-human collaboration. As a result, optimization can yield motions that are legible but not human-like, 344 

deviating from the conventions that humans naturally use when coordinating with each other. We argue 345 

that progress toward human-like legibility requires studying sensorimotor communication in human–human 346 

experiments, where signaling emerges in its most natural form with physical coupling and multimodal feedback. 347 

By grounding legibility in empirically observed patterns, our approach provides a reference for robots to 348 

communicate through the shared object using the same channels humans rely on, and it supports a unified 349 

framework to infer intent from movement, and to shape movement given intent. 350 

It should be noted that communication is inherently bilateral [49], yet our measurements and analyses 351 

primarily characterize how the informed partner’s movement and forces reveal intent, with limited access to 352 

how the uninformed partner contributes to the exchange. In practice, followers may signal uncertainty, confirm 353 

understanding, or actively probe. Such feedback loops are only partially captured when the analysis is centered 354 

on leader-driven cues. Furthermore, while the Random Forest and SHAP analyses identify predictive features, 355 

they do not recover a unique “true” signal. Since many kinematic and force variables are mechanically coupled, 356 

correlated features can trade off in importance estimates. We alleviated this by selecting relatively independent 357 

features, but residual coupling is unavoidable in physical interaction and can vary across workspace regions. 358 

Finally, our paradigm focuses on one type of information asymmetry, where one partner has more information 359 

than the other. However, real collaboration may involve more dynamic information contexts, for instance with 360 

an asymmetry that emerges only mid-trial, or interacting partners having different task-relevant knowledge. 361 

Beyond human-human collaboration, learning sensorimotor communication presents a unique challenge for 362 

human-robot interactions. Unlike verbal communication, it is inherently flexible and adaptive and lacks a 363 

stable mapping from a particular force–motion pattern to its meaning. This makes it challenging to generate 364 

comprehensive descriptions for an AI or a robot to learn from. Recent progress in training robots from large-scale 365 

video data [50] is structurally limited for physical collaboration, where haptic signals such as interaction forces 366 

and limb stiffness are critical, but not visually observable. While robots could learn by directly interacting with 367 

humans, collecting such data is resource-intensive and may pose safety risks during physical interaction. More 368 

importantly, humans are extremely adaptive and may rapidly infer a robot’s limitations before it can learn, 369 

which could lead them to switch to simplified or exaggerated strategies. The robot would thus end up learning 370 

downgraded and suboptimal patterns. 371 

In contrast, by grounding legibility in empirically observed behavioral patterns, our approach provides a 372 

usable reference for deciphering human-human interactions. The decoded communication patterns could inform 373 

the design of robots that communicate intent in a human-compatible way, with potential applications in physical 374 

assistance, rehabilitation, joint training, and sensorimotor augmentation [51]. 375

3.2 Experimental paradigm 407 

Task and conditions: The experimental task required dyads to collaboratively transport the table object 408 

to a target while avoiding obstacles. At the onset of each trial, the table was fixed at the start location and 409

and participants slid the table to the target, while avoiding collisions with the walls or entering decoys. Verbal 413

both participants contributed force in coordination, providing a natural incentive to collaborate. 415 

We designed three block conditions: Symmetric, Asymmetric, and Button Press (Fig. 2B). Conditions differed 416

the Asymmetric and Button Press conditions, only one participant was informed of the green target, and the 419

the dyad only learned the current trial type after the button press. 425

3.5 Decoding Sensorimotor Communication 484 

As shown in the Results, sensorimotor communication during joint object manipulation is expressed through 485 

multimodal, high-dimensional signals. The interpretation of cues is conditional on both task context and the set 486 

of co-occurring cues. Because cue effects can interact nonlinearly, their semantics are not additive. We therefore 487 

adopted an explainable machine learning approach to relate the sensorimotor signals to the inferred target. This 488 

approach comprised two steps: (i) Random Forest models integrated the multidimensional features and produced 489 

a probability distribution over candidate targets, and (ii) SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) quantified the 490 

contribution of each feature to the model output, enabling interpretation of which signals supported each target 491 

prediction and how signals can be shaped to optimize legibility. 492 

3.5.1 Decoding intended target from multimodal cues using Random Forest 493 

In this task, the informed participant conveys the intended target through sensorimotor cues, so the cue 494 

interpretation can be formulated as a classification problem. We used a Random Forest classifier [58] because 495 

ensembles of decision trees can model nonlinear decision boundaries and feature interactions, are well-suited to 496 

high-dimensional sensorimotor signals, and often perform effectively with small-to-moderate training sets. 497 

We trained Random Forest (RF) classifiers using the Scikit-learn library [59] to decode the target from the 498 

instantaneous sensorimotor state at each timestep. Input features comprised table position ( x, y ), orientation 499 

(θ), speed ( ∥v∥), velocity direction ( θv), and the leader’s force ( Fx, F y) and force rate ( ˙∥F∥). The leader was 500 

defined as the participant with higher work share in the Symmetric blocks, and as the informed participant in the 501 

Asymmetric and Button Press blocks. RF models treated each timestep as a sample, output class probabilities 502 

over the four targets, and predicted the target with the highest probability. Data were downsampled to 100 Hz. 503 

Train–test splits were performed at the trial level to avoid temporal leakage. Within each block, each target in 504 

each scene appeared five times and one trial was randomly held out for testing (20%), and the remaining four 505 

trials were used for training. 506 

We trained a general model using data aggregated across all dyads and dyad-specific models using data from 507 

each dyad. Models were trained separately for each scene. Hyperparameters were selected by grid search over 508 

maximum tree depth 6 , 7, 8, 10 , 12 , 15 , 20 , 30 and number of trees 50 , 100 , 200 , 300, using 3-fold cross-validation 509 

and accuracy as the scoring metric. To balance predictive accuracy and model complexity, we set the maximum 510 

tree depth to 10 for the general model and to 8 for dyad-specific models, both using 50 trees. 511 

To visualize how model confidence varied across the workspace, we discretized it into 0 .5 cm × 0.5 cm bins. 512 

For each bin, we averaged the model’s predicted probability for the target across all samples within that bin. 513 

To compare model similarities, and whether different models produced similar predictions for the same 514 

sensorimotor signals, we evaluated each dyad-specific and the general model on a pooled evaluation dataset 515 

containing all dyads and computed pairwise difference between models using the Jensen–Shannon (JS) distance 516 

between their predicted probability vectors. JS distance was computed at each timestep for each pair of models 517 

and then averaged over timesteps and trials. To visualize the JS distances between dyads, a 3D embedding 518 

was generated using metric multidimensional scaling (MDS) [60] based on the dissimilarity matrix using the 519 

Scikit-learn library [59]. This projection maps the high-dimensional dissimilarity matrix into a 3D Euclidean 520 

space, preserving the original distances as closely as possible. 521 

3.6 Interpreting model predictions with SHAP 522 

RF models map high-dimensional sensorimotor cues to a confidence value for each target. However, the mapping 523 

is not directly interpretable, which prevents assessing how individual signals and their interactions contribute 524 

to the model’s prediction. We therefore applied SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to quantify feature 525 

contributions to the RF outputs [39, 40]. SHAP is based on Shapley values from cooperative game theory [61] and 526 

quantifies each feature’s average marginal contribution to the prediction. For each sample, SHAP decomposes 527 

the model output into an expected value (the baseline) plus additive contributions from individual features. A 528 

positive (negative) SHAP value indicates that the corresponding feature increases (decreases) the predicted 529 

probability relative to the baseline, and the magnitude reflects the strength of this influence. SHAP values are 530 

expressed here in the unit of predicted probability. 531

3.7 Drift-Diffusion Models 543 

To model how the uninformed participant accumulated evidence about the target from the evolving sensorimotor 544 

cues, we integrated the Random Forest model with a Drift-Diffusion Model (DDM) [62, 63, 64]. 545 

The DDM describes a decision process in which noisy evidence is integrated over time until it reaches a decision 546 

bound. Classical DDM formulations assume a one-dimensional evidence stream, whereas our sensorimotor 547 

signals are high-dimensional and do not directly represent evidence. We therefore used the RF model to map 548 

the signals to target-specific probability estimates, and treated these probabilities as momentary evidence. For 549 

each target i ∈ { 1, 2, 3, 4}, we defined the momentary evidence as the deviation of the RF output from chance 550 

level: ei(t) = Pi(t) − 0.25. Here Pi(t) is the probability predicted by RF at time t for target i, and 0.25 is the 551 

chance level for four alternatives. When ei(t) > 0, the model accumulated positive evidence in favor of target i,552 

and vice versa. 553

in parallel, one per target. A decision was made when the first accumulator reached the bound a, and the 555 

corresponding target was taken as the model’s predicted choice: 556
