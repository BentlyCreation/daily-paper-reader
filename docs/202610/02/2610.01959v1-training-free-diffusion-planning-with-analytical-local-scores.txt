# ABSTRACT 

Motion planning requires trajectories that are smooth, goal-directed, and collision-free in environments with complex geometric constraints. Recent diffusion-based planners have shown that trajectory generation can be cast as iterative denoising, which has opened the doors to learning-based approaches that can handle multi-modal trajectory distributions and refine entire trajectories. However, such planners require training on large collections of feasible trajectories, rendering them map-specific, and difficult to deploy when high-quality demonstrations are unavailable. This paper introduces a training-free diffusion-based motion planner that replaces learned global trajectory scores with analytical local scores derived from obstacle, smoothness, velocity, and inter-agent feasibility terms. The proposed idea relies on a key observation: the trajectory score can be approximated from local interactions between neighboring waypoints and nearby constraints. This structure exploitation yields a decomposed denoising procedure that retains the optimization structure of classical optimization methods while inheriting the iterative refinement behavior of diffusion models. Experiments on a large collection of complex environments on multi-agent planning tasks show that the proposed analytical score produces smooth and feasible trajectories within very limited computational costs, for example in generating feasible paths for 300 agents in environments with 100 obstacles in under 6 seconds, while avoiding the data requirements of learned diffusion planners. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Motion planning is a fundamental problem in robotics and autonomous systems, where the goal is to compute trajectories that move a system from an initial state to a target state while avoiding collisions with obstacles (LaValle, 2006). In multi-agent settings, this problem becomes substantially more challenging: each robot must satisfy its own motion and safety constraints while also avoiding time-dependent collisions with other agents (Sharon et al., 2015; Ma et al., 2018). The spatio-temporal coupling and nonlinearity of these constraints make multi-agent path planning difficult to solve efficiently, especially as the number of agents and obstacles increases (Liang et al., 2025). Sampling-based planners provide strong completeness properties under suitable assumptions and have been widely used for high-dimensional planning problems. However, they often produce paths in a coarse configuration space that require additional smoothing and can become inefficient in narrow passages or crawded spaces (Kuffner & LaValle, 2000; LaValle, 2006). Optimization-based planners instead formulate motion planning as continuous trajectory optimization, making it possible to incorporate obstacle avoidance, smoothness, and kinematic constraints directly into the objective or feasible set (Kalakrishnan et al., 2011; Mukadam et al., 2018b). However, these methods are often sensitive to initialization and can become expensive in multi-agent settings because collision constraints couple many agents across time. More recently, diffusion models have shown promising results for motion planning by casting trajectory generation as an iterative denoising process that refines noise into trajectories (Liang et al., 2026b; Shaoul et al., 2025). This perspective is attractive because diffusion models refine entire trajectories rather than committing to one waypoint at a time, and can represent multimodal distributions over possible plans (Carvalho et al., 2023). The key challenge, however, is that existing diffusion planners require learning a neural score model over high-dimensional trajectory distributions. This makes them expensive to train, dependent on large collections of feasible demonstrations, and often map- or distribution-specific. Moreover,

Projection-based refinement can improve constraint satisfaction, but it introduces additional computational cost (Liang et al., 2026b; 2025). To address these challenges, this paper develops a training-free diffusion-based planner with fully analytical local scores. The key idea is based on the observation that trajectory refinement has a strong locality of influence (Pan et al., 2012). Although the exact score of a full trajectory distribution can be globally coupled, the update of an individual waypoint is dominated by local temporal and spatial interactions: neighboring waypoints determine smoothness and velocity consistency, nearby obstacles determine collision avoidance, and nearby agents at the same time determine inter-robot separation (Mukadam et al., 2018a; Zucker et al., 2013), as illustrated in Figure 1(b). Motivated by this structure, we introduce a decomposed local score formulation that constructs an analytical score surrogate directly from the planning constraints, rather than learning a neural approximation to the score of a high-dimensional distribution over complete trajectories. The resulting planner starts from a coarse trajectory initialization with fixed endpoints (Figure 1(a)) and repeatedly applies analytical denoising updates under a decreasing noise schedule (Figure 1(c)). Because each update depends only on local trajectory neighborhoods, nearby obstacles, and nearby agents, the method avoids dataset collection and neural-network training while remaining scalable to large multi-agent scenes. 

Contributions. The paper makes the following contributions: (1) It introduces a training-free diffusion planning framework that replaces learned global trajectory scores with analytical score surrogates derived from the structure of the motion planning problem. (2) It develops a decomposed local score formulation in which each waypoint is refined using smoothness, obstacle-avoidance, velocity-feasibility, and inter-agent collision-avoidance terms. (3) It demonstrates across multi-agent path planning benchmarks that the proposed planner achieves high feasibility and computational efficiency, obtaining high success on standard test configurations and scaling to scenes with 300 

agents and 100 obstacles in just a few seconds. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Multi-agent path planning has a long history in robotics, with early methods largely built around sampling-based and optimization-based approaches. Sampling-based planners , approximate the feasible configuration space by drawing samples and connecting them through local steering pro-cedures (Kavraki et al., 1996; Kuffner & LaValle, 2000; LaValle, 2006). These can scale to high-dimensional spaces better than exhaustive search methods, however, they often produce piecewise or fragmented paths that require post-processing, and their performance can degrade in narrow passages or settings where continuous kinodynamic constraints must be enforced along the entire trajectory. A complementary line of work formulates motion planning as trajectory optimization 

problems (Augugliaro et al., 2012; Park et al., 2020; Mellinger et al., 2012). This gives these methods a direct mechanism for incorporating feasibility requirements, but the resulting optimization problems become increasingly difficult in multi-agent settings, where collision constraints couple agents across

To improve computational tractability, several optimization-based planners replace hard feasibility constraints with differentiable costs or penalty terms (Ratliff et al., 2009; Kalakrishnan et al., 2011; Mukadam et al., 2018b), but they are often sensitive to initialization and can struggle in environments with many obstacles or agents. In contrast, our method formulates planning as an iterative denoising process driven by analytical local scores, avoiding dense state-space exploration while also avoiding repeated global trajectory optimization. 

Score-based Generative Models for Motion Planning . Recent advancements in generative models offer have opened new avenues for solving motion planning problems by treating feasible trajectories as samples from a distribution and generating them through iterative denoising (Song et al., 2020). Diffusion planners are attractive because they refine whole trajectories at once and can represent multimodal plans in dense environments (Carvalho et al., 2023; Shaoul et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2024). However, they typically require learning a neural score over high-dimensional trajectory distributions, which depends on large collections of feasible demonstrations and does not guarantee constraint satisfaction. To improve feasibility, recent work has added gradient-based guidance during sampling, control barrier and Lyapunov functions, or projection-based refinement after denoising (Carvalho et al., 2023; Mizuta & Leung, 2024; Shaoul et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2026b; 2025; Christopher et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2026a). These Both retain learned generative models; TFDP constructs its refinement field directly from smoothness and feasibility factors. The method proposed in this work, instead, replaces the learned global score with analytical local score components derived directly from smoothness, velocity, obstacle-avoidance, and collision constraints. This yields a training-free planner that preserves the iterative refinement behavior of diffusion models while making denoising updates fast and tied to the geometry of the environment.

Diffusion models for motion planning. Score-based diffusion models define a forward noising process and a reverse sampling process (Song et al., 2020). In the forward process, samples from a data distribution are gradually perturbed with Gaussian noise. Let π denote a trajectory, and let 

p0(π | O , s, g ) denote the distribution of feasible trajectories conditioned on the obstacle set O, start state s, and goal state g. At noise level σ, the forward process induces a noise-smoothed distribution 

pσ by πσ = π + σϵ , with ϵ ∼ N (0 , I ). The reverse process then generates samples by moving noisy trajectories back toward the feasible trajectory distribution. This reverse process is driven by the score function ∇π log pσ (π | O , s, g ), which points in the direction of increasing probability under the noise-smoothed distribution. In learned diffusion planners, feasible trajectories are treated as data samples and used to train a neural network sθ to approximate this score function (Carvalho et al., 2023; Shaoul et al., 2025; Liang et al., 2025): 

sθ (π; O, s, g ). ≈ ∇ π log pσ (π | O , s, g ) (2)

Prior diffu-sion planners often add guidance terms during sampling to penalize obstacle penetration, inter-agent collisions, or trajectory roughness (Carvalho et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2025; 2026b). However, the underlying model still requires learning a global score over high-dimensional trajectory distributions, which is expensive and depends on access to feasible training trajectories. Next, the paper introduces a method that avoids learning such a global score model altogether.

The local score decomposition approximates the noisy trajectory score and does not imply exact conditional independence between distant waypoints. For Gaussian trajectory priors with local interactions, zeros in the precision matrix encode conditional independencies, and adding isotropic diffusion noise changes the precision structure. At small noise levels, indirect dependencies between distant variables are higher-order effects relative to the direct neighbor interactions. This motivates using short score windows in low-noise denoising regimes and considering larger windows at higher noise levels. # 5 EXPERIMENTS 

Settings. To evaluate the performance of various multi-agent path planning algorithms, we design two experimental settings targeting different aspects of the problem: 

1. Feasibility, Efficiency, Quality: We adopt a recent multi-agent path planning benchmark (Liang et al., 2026b) which includes four representative map types: random maps with varying obstacle densities (basic and dense), and structured maps mimicking real-world environments (room and shelf layouts). For each map type, we consider three robot counts (6, 12, and 18), and evaluate across 25 instances with different configurations. Figure 3 illustrates the benchmark maps and representative 

18 -robot trajectories found by our method. 

2. Scalability: To test scalability, we use a large-scale map containing over 100 heterogeneous obstacles. This setup is challenging and supports scenarios with up to 300 robots, largely beyond what existing diffusion methods handle. 

Evaluation Metrics. Different algorithms are empirically evaluated based on Success rate, Running time, and Path Length. The Success rate indicates the proportion of test cases solved without collisions and within the time limit (900 seconds), Running time measures the computational efficiency required to generate a solution, Path Length evaluates the quality of generated trajectories. 

Competing Methods. Our proposed TFDP is compared against diffusion-based and classical baseline methods: (1) Multi-robot motion planning diffusion (MMD) : A method that integrates diffusion models with a classical search-based MAPF algorithm to generate multi-agent trajectories under soft collision constraints (Shaoul et al., 2025). (2) Simultaneous multi-robot motion planning diffusion (SMD) : It integrates diffusion models with constrained optimization to generate collision-free trajectories for multi-agent path planning (Liang et al., 2025). (3) Discrete-guided diffusion (DGD) : This approach decomposes the original multi-agent path planning problem and leverages MAPF-derived spatiotemporal dependencies to guide diffusion models in generating trajectories that satisfy hard feasibility constraints. (Liang et al., 2026b). (4) Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) : A classical sampling-based method whose updates admit a score-ascent interpretation (Xue et al., 2024). This connection motivates its use as a baseline: MPPI estimates update directions from sampled perturbations, while TFDP uses analytical approximations to local scores. For MMD, SMD, and DGD, we follow the hyperparameter settings reported in their original papers. For MPPI, we treat the MPPI σ the same as diffusion σ and use the our approach’s annealed energy landscapes as the optimization objective. We generate random start and goal positions for each robot, ensuring that they are not in collision with obstacles or each other. Importantly, TFDP produces valid plans for such maps in 5.39 seconds, on average on an A100 GPU. None of the diffusion baselines tested were able to handle this scale, and MPPI fails to find feasible plans for more than 50 robots. Figure 6 in Appendix A.15 shows a generated plan. We furthertest two deadlock scenarios in which robots must pass through a wall opening only wide enough for one robot (see Appendix A.12, Figure 4). Here, TFDP achieves much higher success than MPPI across the tested robot counts, indicating an advantage when passage through a shared bottleneck requires coordination. 

# 6 CONCLUSION 

This paper presented a training-free analytical diffusion planner for path finding and multi-agent path planning. The method replaces learned full-trajectory scores with decomposed analytical scores derived from smoothness, obstacle, velocity, and inter-agent feasibility terms. The analysis and experiments show that local temporal neighborhoods capture most of the useful score information needed for denoising, enabling efficient trajectory refinement without demonstrations or map-specific training. Future work will extend the method to richer kinodynamic models, more challenging maze-like environments, and large-scale multi-agent benchmarks. Note that, although this is standard to test multi-agent planning on 2D environments, it is interesting to consider extensions to higher dimensions, which we believe will be a natural next step. We also assume a centralized planner and perfect plan executions, and so it is interesting to consider how approaches involving diffusion-like sampling processes could be extended to decentralized planning problems.

26 A.16 OPTIMIZATION TIMELINES 

Figure 7 tracks the fraction of feasible plans over 250 refinement iterations on each benchmark map. TFDP uses η = 0 .5; the two CHOMP-style SDF variants use α = 0 .05 with η ∈ { 0.2, 0.5}. SDF guidance produces feasible plans earlier, while TFDP improves sharply during the middle of the schedule and finishes with the highest feasible fraction on all four maps. On Dense, the final fractions are approximately 0.96 for TFDP and 0.84 for both SDF variants; on Room, they are approximately 

0.98 , 0.86 , and 0.73 for TFDP and SDF with η = 0 .5 and 0.2, respectively. These curves show why final feasibility and early progress need to be assessed separately. Since the horizontal axis counts iterations, the figure describes refinement behavior rather than wall-clock efficiency.
